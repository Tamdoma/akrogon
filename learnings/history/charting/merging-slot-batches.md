# Merging slot A and slot B question batches

## What was not good
The first merges were done by feel. The operator could not tell which question came from which slot, or why one of slot B's questions was missing.

## What the updated way does
Merge by the thing decided, not by wording. Practitioner backing, tier 1: the Delphi method (Dalkey and Helmer, RAND, 1963) collects independent positions blind, then a synthesizer combines them and keeps disagreement visible instead of averaging it away.

1. Pair questions that decide the same thing into one question. Union the options.
2. When the slots recommend different options, both appear as options. Slot A's evidence picks which is first, and the batch names the other as slot B's pick. Disagreement is the signal that the fork is real.
3. A pitfall one slot raises that the other slot has no option for becomes its own question.
4. A question that belongs to another decision moves to that decision's file, never silently dropped.
5. A question that only applies under one answer of another question waits for the next round, and the batch says so.
6. Both slots' sources are cited. Where the sources conflict, the conflict goes in the pitfalls line.
7. Provenance goes in the decision file's Findings, one line per question naming its origin. The batch itself stays clean.

## Why
The operator decides from the batch. Anything hidden in the merge is a decision the agent took without asking, which breaks the grilling type.

## How it gets improved
When the skill is rewritten, these seven steps become the merge section of the slot protocol. If the operator asks "where did this question come from" again, the provenance line was missing.
